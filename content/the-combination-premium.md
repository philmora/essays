If depth alone isn't the moat anymore, what is?

The answer is surprisingly mathematical: **combinations**.

## § 01 · Why combinations beat depth.

Every major AI model is trained on roughly the same corpus — the documented knowledge of humanity. Within any single domain, AI has seen more than any human expert ever could.

But ask it to see that a problem in healthcare operations shares a deep structure with a problem in video game design — and it struggles. Not because it lacks information, but because that specific connection doesn't exist anywhere it learned from.

Novel combinations are, by definition, rare in training data.

This is where human competitive advantage lives now: in holding multiple domains deeply enough to see connections that haven't been documented yet.

The person who understands both behavioral economics and product design sees opportunities invisible to specialists in either field. The engineer who studied organizational psychology builds systems that pure technologists can't conceive. The healthcare executive who spent years in gaming understands engagement and habit formation in ways their peers don't.

The insight doesn't come from depth in one field. It comes from fluency across fields that creates novel intersections.

## § 02 · The math of combinations.

Here's what makes this powerful: combinations scale exponentially.

Two domains give you one intersection. Three domains give you three intersections. Four domains give you six. Five give you ten.

And "intersections" undersells it. Each combination isn't just additive — it's multiplicative. The person who understands engineering, psychology, and economics sees things that none of those three specialists can see individually, because the *three-way* intersection is where the rarest insights live.

The combinations compound.

This is why polymaths are suddenly more valuable than hyperspecialists. Not because breadth is inherently better than depth — but because the right breadth creates exponentially more surface area for novel insight. And novel insight is exactly what AI struggles with.

The arithmetic is unkind to the career path most people were told to follow.

## § 03 · What "fluency" actually requires.

This isn't about being a dilettante. Shallow knowledge of many things is just trivia.

Fluency means you can hold a real conversation with an expert. You understand the core frameworks, the key debates, the common failure modes. You know enough to ask the questions that matter — and enough to recognize when an answer is off.

You don't need to be world-class in any single domain. But you need genuine competence — enough to recognize patterns, apply concepts, and see when something from Domain A might solve a problem in Domain B.

The fluency test: *Can you read the serious literature in a field and follow the arguments?* Can you spot when someone is oversimplifying? Do you know what the practitioners actually argue about?

If yes, you're fluent. If you can only recite surface facts, you're not there yet.

The gap between "I read a summary of this field" and "I can hold a real argument with someone who works in it" is the entire premium. That's where the value lives.

## § 04 · How to choose which combinations to build.

Not all combinations are equally valuable. Some are common — the engineer who can communicate, the designer who understands business. These are table stakes now. They used to be differentiators a decade ago; now they're the baseline.

The valuable combinations have three characteristics:

1. **They're rare.** If lots of people have the same combination, it's not a moat. "Healthcare + gaming" is rarer than "business + marketing."
2. **They're relevant.** The combination needs to apply to problems that matter. Poetry + advanced mathematics is rare but has limited application. Finance + behavioral science creates value in multiple contexts.
3. **They're hard to assemble.** The best combinations require genuine investment to build fluency. If someone can acquire your combination in a weekend, it's not defensible.

The sweet spot: combinations that don't obviously belong together, that apply to real problems, and that require enough depth in each that most people won't bother.

For me personally: engineering + product + healthcare + AI agents. Each of those four took years of work. The four-way combination is roughly the entire scope of what I do at Machinify. I don't know another person with exactly that combination, which is the point.

## § 05 · How to actually build fluency in a new domain.

This is where most advice gets vague. "Become a polymath" isn't actionable. Here's what actually works:

**Start with a real problem, not a curriculum.** Don't "learn finance." Instead, try to understand a specific question: why do startups use convertible notes? What makes a balance sheet healthy? Curiosity about a specific problem pulls you into a domain faster than a reading list.

**Find the canonical sources.** Every field has 3–5 books that practitioners actually respect. Find them. Read them. Skip the pop-science summaries — they give you vocabulary without understanding. Bonus points for reading the sources practitioners *disagree* about, because that's where the live debates are.

**Learn the arguments, not just the answers.** Fluency means understanding what experts disagree about. What are the competing schools of thought? What's controversial? What's settled? This is where real understanding lives. Anyone can memorize answers. Practitioners have opinions.

**Build something.** Nothing accelerates fluency like trying to apply knowledge. Build a financial model. Design an experiment. Write something that practitioners would critique. The feedback loop is where fluency solidifies, and it's the step most self-study skips.

**Find a practitioner who'll talk to you.** One conversation with someone who actually works in a field is worth ten books. They'll tell you what the books get wrong, what actually matters, and what's changing. This step is underrated; it's usually the difference between "I read about this field" and "I understand this field."

**Timeline:** genuine fluency in a new domain takes 6–18 months of serious engagement. Not full-time — but consistent, focused effort. Weekends and evenings compound. Two hours a week for a year is enough to change your worldview in a field; skipping weeks is enough to prevent it.

## § 06 · The hiring implication.

If you're building a team, stop optimizing for depth within defined roles.

Traditional hiring: *"We need a senior backend engineer." "We need a healthcare regulatory expert."*

That logic produces specialists competing with AI on AI's home turf — and the market is about to stop paying a premium for it.

Different question: *"Who has an unusual combination of fluencies that would be valuable here — even if they don't fit a standard job description?"*

The people with weird backgrounds — the career changers, the multi-hyphenates, the ones whose resumes don't tell a clean story — might be exactly the people whose combinations create the most value. The recruiting system that screens them out on "no direct experience" is the recruiting system that hands the moat to your competitors.

Stop screening for clean stories. Start screening for interesting intersections.

## § 07 · The personal implication.

If your career has felt scattered — if you've been told to focus, to pick a lane, to stop chasing shiny objects — consider the possibility that you were building something valuable.

Not despite the zigzags. *Because* of them.

Each domain you've explored is a potential connection point. Each "distraction" is raw material for combinations that don't exist yet. The advice to pick a lane was good advice for the old economy. In the new one, it's a recipe for building a career that AI will absorb inside of five years.

The strategic question isn't whether to specialize or generalize. It's: *which combination of fluencies would be rare, valuable, and hard to replicate — and are you going deep enough in each to make the connections real?*

AI has all the information. It doesn't have your specific combination of experiences. That's not a bug in your career — it's the feature.

Go build the combination. The compounding favors you.
